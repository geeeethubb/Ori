# Ori: your phone, your story

Storytelling brief and speaker script for the Kearney hackathon pitch. Prepared November 15, 2026.

This document governs the narrative, audience-facing words, evidence, demo story, and emotional progression. It contains no application implementation instructions. The separate integration brief governs the website. **Section 8, How we used Velocity AI, is a required part of the main presentation. Keep it visible and spoken.**

## The story in one paragraph

People collect sparks all day: a saved reel, a sentence that stays with them, a small frustration, a possibility they cannot quite articulate. Turning those sparks into something they consistently do takes several kinds of work. Ori's vision is an AI-native phone experience that helps with that work: a companion holds the context you choose to share, helps you discover what you want, and shapes personal apps and workspaces that evolve with you. We begin with busy, curious UIUC students whom we can reach and learn from directly. Our customer discovery gives us an initial signal; our next job is to earn repeat use. Velocity AI helped us turn broad assumptions into specific product decisions and behavioral tests.

The emotional journey is **recognition → understanding → possibility → visible proof → confidence in execution**. The audience should leave remembering a spark becoming something useful that they can return to.

## Positioning and one-liners

**Recommended slide slogan:** “Your phone, your story.”

**Recommended spoken introduction:** “We're building Ori: an AI-native phone experience where little sparks become personal apps that grow with you, starting on the phone you already own.”

**Shorter spoken alternative:** “Ori is the AI-native phone, with apps shaped around your interests and everyday life.” Follow immediately with the clarification that this starts as software on an existing phone.

Other short slide options:

- “Your sparks. Your story. Ori.”
- “Give your sparks somewhere to grow.”
- “A little spark. A world of your own.”
- “Apps that grow with you.”

“Story” supplies a natural near-rhyme with Ori. Do not sacrifice clarity for a forced rhyme. Use “AI-native” once to establish the ambition, then make its value concrete: less setup, help clarifying a need, a useful interface, retained progress, and an easier return tomorrow. Avoid promising effortless behavior change or instant results for every person.

## 2. Research and customer discovery: a broad gap, then a local insight

### Scene 2 — Wanting is only the beginning.

**On screen: two clearly separate research cards**

**47.6%**
Of people intending to be physically active, nearly half did not follow through.

Small source: Feil et al., 2023; meta-analysis, 25 samples, 29,600 participants overall. Physical activity specifically. [R1]

**47 experiments**
Strengthening intention produced a smaller change in behavior.

Small source: Webb & Sheeran, 2006; effects on intention d = 0.66 and behavior d = 0.36. [R2]

**Spoken track**

> This gap is bigger than a forgotten bookmark. A meta-analysis of physical activity found that nearly half of people who intended to act did not follow through. Wanting something and consistently doing it are different problems.

**Story beat:** The research establishes that follow-through is a serious, studied problem. It does not establish how many people need Ori. Keep the exercise domain visible beside the percentage. The two cards are separate findings, not a combined statistic.

### Scene 3 — 21 of 26 students said yes.

**On screen**

> “Do you struggle to turn your ideas into something consistent?”
>
> **21 of 26 said yes.**
>
> One conversation changed where we thought the problem began.

Footnote: “Founder-reported, informal discovery with 26 UIUC students; a convenience sample. 21/26 = 80.8%. Early local signal, not a campus-wide estimate.”

**Spoken track**

> We asked 26 students whether they struggle to turn their ideas into something consistent. Twenty-one said yes. But a friend explained that sometimes she struggles to pinpoint the idea itself. Maybe all you have is a saved reel, a sentence that stayed with you, or a frustration you cannot yet explain.

**Story beat:** First show the count. Then shift attention to the friend's insight. A reel thumbnail, a torn-out sentence, and an unfinished note make the insight human. Label examples as illustrative; do not make a fabricated survey transcript.

**Expanded story, for notes or a longer presentation**

> We asked people whether they struggle to turn their ideas into something consistent. Twenty-one of twenty-six said yes.
>
> But one response made us rethink where the problem begins.
>
> A friend explained that she sometimes struggles to pinpoint what the idea even is.
>
> Maybe it starts with an Instagram reel you saved because you wanted to try something. A sentence you read that stayed with you. Or a frustration you haven't quite put into words.
>
> You can bring that to Ori.
>
> Ori is your companion for holding onto those sparks, connecting them with what you've shared, and helping you explore what they could become. As you react, clarify, and change your mind, Ori helps shape a next step around you.
>
> You don't need a fully formed idea to begin. You just need somewhere to bring it.

**What changed because of discovery:** Our design starts before a precise prompt or app specification. The friend gave us a reason to investigate the first transition more closely. We have not measured how many people stall at each stage.

**Placement decision:** Keep this finding near the opening, after the external research. It supplies the human discovery behind the product. Refer to this same student audience again in GTM to explain the first pilot. Do not save the entire discovery story for GTM or present the count twice as two separate validations.

## 3. The five stages: the central explanation

### Scene 4 — There is work between a spark and a habit.

**On screen**

| Stage | Human task | Ori's intended role |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Feel it | Something interests or bothers you. | Capture the spark with the context you choose to share. |
| 2. Name it | Put that interest or need into words. | Ask a useful question; reflect possible meanings. |
| 3. Imagine it | Work out what an ideal outcome would feel like. | Explore several useful directions. |
| 4. Shape it | Turn the desired outcome into a concrete tool or workflow. | Propose a personal app or adapt an existing one. |
| 5. Build and iterate | Use it, learn, and change it. | Support useful actions and revisions while retaining progress. |

Bottom line: **“Start wherever you are. Keep growing from there.”**

**Spoken track**

> We see five stages: feel a spark, name the need, imagine what would help, shape a solution, then build and iterate. You can get stuck between any of them. Ori helps carry the middle steps, whether you arrive with a detailed plan or just “something about this interests me.”

**Story beat:** Reveal the five stages one at a time. Emphasize the first transition when recalling the friend, then show possible gaps across the whole path. A line returns from stage five to earlier stages: using something changes what you want. This is a product framework, not a scientifically validated five-stage model.

**The ideal solution:** You should be able to arrive uncertain, explore without committing, turn useful context into something actionable, and keep changing the result without starting over. Ori reduces the work around acting; the person still chooses, practices, explores, and does the real-world work.

Do not say “most students are stuck between one and two,” “Ori completes all five instantly,” or “every spark needs an app.” Sometimes the right outcome is saving a thought, asking a question, or using a tool that already exists. That choice is part of Ori's intelligence.

## 4. Solution and demo: one spark, one evolving app

### Scene 5 — Bring the spark. We will shape what comes next.

**On screen**

> A reel. A sentence. A small frustration.
>
> **Keep the context. Explore the possibility. Shape the next step.**

Secondary labels: “Companion → Playground → Your apps.”

**Spoken track**

> Share a thought, screenshot, or context from a conversation. Ori helps keep and explore it. When a tool would help, that context becomes a personal workspace you can use and keep shaping.

**Story beat:** Give the mood board room to breathe here. Three imperfect scraps become one coherent direction, not an instant polished app. Show one small clarification that changes the proposal. A companion earns its role by remembering relevant, user-chosen context and responding to correction.

**Scope for the story:** Instagram and LinkedIn are sources a user might share from. Sharing text, a screenshot, or a link does not imply direct account access. AI conversation context means user-selected, reviewed imports unless an actual connector is verified. If a link's content cannot be read, Ori should say so and use the user's explanation. Native share-sheet integration and broad background automation remain future capabilities unless demonstrated.

## 5. Why Ori: make “AI-native phone” mean something

### Scene 9 — An app store built around you.

**On screen**

> Your context → your workspace → your next step.

Three benefits:

- **Begin before the brief.** A vague interest is enough to start exploring.
- **Get an interface that fits.** A photo gallery, a practice session, a follow-up queue.
- **Keep a growing collection.** Return to useful tools and adapt them with your progress intact.

**Spoken track**

> Ori's ambition is an AI-native phone and a personal app store built around you. The experience connects discovery, an interface that fits the activity, and an app you can return to as your needs change.

**Expanded competitive argument**

Our proposed advantage is continuity across the whole journey, plus a focused way to learn from users. App generation alone is easy to demonstrate and increasingly common. The valuable outcome is less work between an uncertain spark and a tool someone repeatedly uses.

| Alternative | What a person can already get | What Ori must earn |
|---|---|---|
| General AI assistants and artifact tools | Help thinking, generating content, and building interactive tools; some already offer storage and integrations. | Less setup across repeated capture, clarification, personal use, and revision. |
| AI app builders | A path from an app request to software. | A useful beginning before the person knows which app to request. |
| Notes, saved posts, bookmarks | A familiar, easy place to keep a spark. | A worthwhile next action without making capture burdensome. |
| Existing specialist apps | Polished, established workflows. | Personal fit strong enough to justify a new tool, or the judgment to reuse an existing Ori app. |

This is positioning, not a verified feature-by-feature superiority chart. For example, Claude's official artifact documentation already describes persistent storage and app connections [R4]. Do not claim that competitors only chat, cannot save data, or cannot build personal apps.

**Defensibility, if asked:** “We do not have a proven moat. Our starting advantage is access to users we can observe closely. We want to build a better understanding of which interventions turn different sparks into useful repeat behavior, and encode that learning into the product. We have to prove that it improves outcomes and becomes difficult to reproduce.” Stored context and a cute companion alone are not defensibility. Explain how users retain control over their own records; dependence on trapped data is not the story.

### Scene 10 — Personal experiences. A shared foundation.

**On screen**

> Shared capabilities. Distinct personal apps.
>
> Useful actions • durable records • careful revisions

**Spoken track**

> The technical approach is a shared set of supported app capabilities, shaped into different experiences. The hard requirement is continuity: a design change must preserve the records that make an app useful.

**Story beat:** Show Light, Riff, and People with visibly different central interactions over one shared foundation. Explain feasibility at the level an investor or judge needs. The slide describes the architecture approach; current code and a working recording determine what can be claimed as implemented.

## 6. GTM: founder access becomes a learning advantage

### Scene 11 — Our first users are people we can sit beside.

**On screen**

> Busy students. Many interests. Limited time to follow through.
>
> **150 invitations → 50 enrolled → 30 activated → 15 active in week four**
>
> Proposed pilot targets, not achieved traction.

Founder access strip:

- **Founders – Illinois Entrepreneurs:** President.
- **IBC:** Project Manager.
- **Grainger + Gies:** engineering student in the Hoeft T&M program.
- **Friend introductions:** OTCR, CUBE, Venture Capital Association (VCA), and business fraternities.

**Spoken track**

> I run Founders at UIUC, am a PM at IBC, and bridge Grainger and Gies through T&M. These are people I can sit beside. We will invite 150 students toward a 50-person pilot, observe the first ten, and target 30 later-day actions and 15 active users in week four.

**Story beat:** Show the founder inside this community, then turn relationships into a concrete experiment. Do not imply that every organization has endorsed Ori or granted distribution access. Personal access is credible because it enables conversations and observation; it is not a guaranteed acquisition channel.

**Why this audience, beyond convenience:** Curious students combine academic work, projects, clubs, career opportunities, and personal interests. That makes them a plausible starting population for tools that carry context between activities. Our 21/26 discovery supports investigating this local need. Separately, Pew's 2026 survey found that 61% of U.S. adults ages 18–29 ever use ChatGPT [R3]. That supports familiarity with conversational AI, not demand for Ori or a student market-size estimate.

**Concrete recruitment plan — proposed allocations**

| Channel | Unique invitations | Access |
|---|---:|---|
| Founders | 50 | Direct founder relationships |
| T&M | 25 | Direct program relationships |
| IBC | 25 | Direct consulting relationships |
| Friends in OTCR, CUBE, VCA, business fraternities | 50 | Introductions to request |
| **Total** | **150** | Deduplicate people across organizations |

150 is an outreach capacity target, not a measured reachable audience. 50 enrollments assumes roughly one-third accept; we must measure that conversion. Start with ten 20–30 minute observed sessions, approximately 3.3–5 hours of observation plus preparation and follow-up. Avoid claiming zero acquisition cost: founder time counts.

**Initial test promise:** “Bring one follow-up you don't want to lose. Turn it into a personal tool and use it again next week.” Recruit students with at least two real follow-ups in the next month. Use People for a repeatable first behavior; let the wider Ori vision remain broader than this one workflow. Do not imply all 26 discovery participants have this specific need.

**Definitions that make the funnel meaningful**

- Enrolled: 50 distinct consenting pilot users who begin onboarding.
- Activated: a meaningful record action on a later day during the first 14 days, such as completing or editing a real follow-up. Creating an app or opening a page alone does not count. Target: 30/50.
- Week-four active: at least one meaningful record action during days 22–28. Target: 15/50, with all enrolled users as denominator. Track prompted versus unprompted returns.
- Day-14 trial expiry changes who has access. Report conversion, paid access, and week-four use together; do not attribute all nonuse to product disengagement.
- Learning decision: continue this wedge if activation is at least 30/50 and week-four use at least 15/50; revise and retest if week-four use is 10–14/50, or if activation is below 30/50 despite week-four use of at least 15/50; reconsider the wedge if week-four use stays below 10/50 after core failures are corrected. A broken journey first calls for a repair, not a market conclusion.

**The next 50:** Test whether useful app blueprints and personal referrals can recruit a second cohort with less founder help. Sharing a blueprint must not share the creator's private records. This is a proposed growth mechanism. Compare invitations, enrollment, useful actions, retained use, and founder minutes per user between cohorts.

**Expansion story:** UIUC is the first learning environment, followed by comparable student communities and then people managing several interests and commitments. Expansion depends on retained use and payment. Do not invent a multibillion-dollar TAM by multiplying a broad intention statistic by subscription price.

## 7. Business model: commercial potential with a measurable next step

### Scene 12 — Earn the return visit. Then earn the subscription.

**On screen**

> **14 days free → $7.50/month for two months → $15/month**
>
> Test useful returns, actual payment, and serving cost.

Footer: “Pricing hypothesis. Conversion, renewal, and margins not yet established.”

**Spoken track**

> Our pricing experiment is a 14-day trial, then seven-fifty a month for two months, then fifteen. We need to earn payment through continued usefulness. We will measure real charges, meaningful use, and serving costs, then test full-price renewal after the introductory period.

**Commercial talking points:** People would pay for a collection that stays useful: creation, adaptation, their records, and dependable access. That is the hypothesis, not a validated willingness to pay. AI usage, failed generations, hosting, storage, and human support all affect economics. Track actual costs before claiming a margin. Do not describe access as unlimited without a tested policy and cost model.

The eight-week pilot can test activation, week-four use, introductory payment, and cost. It cannot establish renewal after two discounted months and the first full-price month. Follow the cohort through the first $15 charge and then the next $15 renewal; do not call either a completed result before it happens.

For scale arithmetic only: 1,000 retained subscribers paying $15 would produce $15,000 monthly subscription revenue before costs. This is a scenario, not a forecast, TAM, or target claimed as achieved. Keep it in Q&A unless a market-sizing question requires it.

## 8. How we used Velocity AI — required main-pitch section

### Scene 13 — Better assumptions. Sharper behavioral tests.

**This section is core to the Kearney hackathon pitch. Keep it in the main sequence, with an explicit Velocity AI headline and spoken attribution. Do not move it to an appendix or reduce it to a logo.**

**On screen**

> **How we used Velocity AI**
>
> Four research rounds + a revised hackathon canvas + a final commercial review.

| Assumption we challenged | Decision the research shaped |
|---|---|
| Every spark needs a new app. | Save, explore, reuse, revise, or create. |
| Generating a screen makes an app useful. | Give each activity a real action; distinguish targets from recorded behavior. |
| Changing an app is just changing its appearance. | Preserve records and field meaning through revisions and undo. |
| App creation proves demand. | Test meaningful return use, payment, and serving costs. |

Closing line: **“From an assumption to a test we can put in front of a person.”**

**Spoken track**

> We used Velocity across four research rounds and a revised hackathon canvas. It helped us choose when to save, reuse, or build; distinguish planned practice from actual practice; and specify revisions that preserve records. Our commercial review sharpened the return-use and payment tests. Velocity helps us get from an assumption to a concrete test, then do what doesn't scale: sit beside students and observe real behavior.

**The entrepreneurial point:** Velocity helps shorten the preparation between an uncertain assumption and a testable decision. The leverage is spending less effort debating a vague concept and more effort learning with actual people. We have not measured a time-saving percentage. AI-generated research and fictional fixtures do not validate customer behavior. Human observation and actual use do that.

**Expanded account, for speaker notes and judge questions**

The earlier working name in the records is “Personal AI Playground”; these are Ori's research artifacts. The handoff documents a saved Build Plan source, a concept in the Kearney workspace, attached research, four research rounds, and a generated then revised Hackathon Decision & Demo Canvas. A later commercial review examined the first-user experiment, competitors, pricing, and costs. That is substantive product and business work, not a last-minute mention of the sponsor's tool.

| Documented activity | What it produced | Why it matters to the customer | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round 1: intent-to-proposal research | Routing choices, source grounding, fictional examples and negative cases. | Ori can explore or save a spark without forcing an app. | Actual research output; early examples were later corrected. |
| Round 2: capability and design mapping | Text captures; a distinction between using an existing app and changing its design; different gallery/session/queue experiences. | Logging a follow-up should not redesign a workspace. **Three examples worth explaining aloud if asked**

1. **Reuse instead of clutter.** A new follow-up should go into the right existing People app. Success is a useful action, not another generated icon.
2. **Preserve the person's progress.** A user should be able to change an app without losing an observation, a session, or a task. Planned duration and measured activity must stay distinct.
3. **Make the experiment behavioral.** Ask whether someone returns on a later day and completes a real action, then whether they pay and renew. Generated apps and enthusiastic reactions are weaker evidence.

**What we can honestly attribute:** Velocity informed the decision workflow, capability mapping, failure cases, data-preservation requirements, and demo/validation plan. The original research handoff did not edit or test the product repository. A later review reports some implementation contributions, but current implementation needs its own evidence. Prefer “shaped,” “specified,” and “helped us test the assumption” over “Velocity built and validated Ori.”

**Do we need to use Velocity more?** The documented contribution is already substantial. A useful next run would take actual prototype recordings and observed pilot sessions, compare them against the existing criteria, and revise one important assumption. The handoff identifies a pending Prompt C for this kind of evidence-based review. Do not run more generic ideation merely to increase a count, and do not count a pending run as completed.
