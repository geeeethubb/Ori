2. Customer discovery, the longer story

Expanded narration for a longer telling or for questions. The friend’s words are paraphrased; the reel, the sentence and the frustration are illustrative.

We asked people whether they struggle to turn their ideas into something consistent. Twenty-one of twenty-six said yes.

But one response made us rethink where the problem begins.

A friend explained that she sometimes struggles to pinpoint what the idea even is.

Maybe it starts with an Instagram reel you saved because you wanted to try something. A sentence you read that stayed with you. Or a frustration you haven’t quite put into words.

You can bring that to Ori.

Ori is your companion for holding onto those sparks, connecting them with what you’ve shared, and helping you explore what they could become. As you react, clarify, and change your mind, Ori helps shape a next step around you.

You don’t need a fully formed idea to begin. You just need somewhere to bring it.

What changed because of discovery. Our design starts before a precise prompt or app specification. The friend gave us a reason to look closely at the first transition. We have not measured how many people stall at each stage.

The number. 21 of 26 said yes (21/26 = 80.8%). Founder-reported, informal, a convenience sample of UIUC students: an early local signal, not a campus-wide estimate and not a figure for college students in general.

3. The five stages, complete

StageThe human taskOri’s intended role
1. Feel itSomething interests or bothers you.Capture the spark with the context you choose to share.
2. Name itPut that interest or need into words.Ask a useful question; reflect possible meanings.
3. Imagine itWork out what an ideal outcome would feel like.Explore several useful directions.
4. Shape itTurn the desired outcome into a concrete tool or workflow.Propose a personal app or adapt an existing one.
5. Build and iterateUse it, learn, and change it.Support useful actions and revisions while retaining progress.

Stage five loops back: using something changes what you want. Inside stage five, the demo shows a small iteration loop: Ask → Review → Keep going. Start wherever you are. Keep growing from there.

The ideal solution. You should be able to arrive uncertain, explore without committing, turn useful context into something actionable, and keep changing the result without starting over. Ori reduces the work around acting; the person still chooses, practices, explores, and does the real-world work.

Not every spark needs an app. Sometimes the right outcome is saving a thought, asking a question, or using a tool that already exists. That choice is part of Ori’s intelligence.

A product framework, not a scientifically validated five-stage model.

5. Positioning and defensibility

Our proposed advantage is continuity across the whole journey, plus a focused way to learn from users. App generation alone is easy to demonstrate and increasingly common. The valuable outcome is less work between an uncertain spark and a tool someone uses again and again.

AlternativeWhat a person can already getWhat Ori must earn
General AI assistants and artifact toolsHelp thinking, generating content and building interactive tools; some already offer storage and integrations.Less setup across repeated capture, clarification, personal use and revision.
AI app buildersA path from an app request to software.A useful beginning before the person knows which app to request.
Notes, saved posts, bookmarksA familiar, easy place to keep a spark.A worthwhile next action without making capture burdensome.
Existing specialist appsPolished, established workflows.Personal fit strong enough to justify a new tool, or the judgment to reuse an existing Ori app.

Positioning, not a verified feature-by-feature comparison. Competitors can chat, save data and build personal apps: Claude’s own artifact documentation describes persistent storage and connected apps [R4].

Defensibility, if asked. “We do not have a proven moat. Our starting advantage is access to users we can observe closely. We want to build a better understanding of which interventions turn different sparks into useful repeat behavior, and encode that learning into the product. We have to prove that it improves outcomes and becomes difficult to reproduce.” Stored context and a friendly companion alone are not defensibility. People should keep control of their own records; trapped data is not the story.

The pilot, proposed targets. 150 invitations → 50 enrolled → 30 later-day useful actions → 15 week-four active users. We observe the first ten sessions (20–30 minutes each: about 3.3–5 hours of observation plus preparation and follow-up). 150 is an outreach capacity target, not a measured reachable audience; 50 enrollments assumes roughly one in three accept, which we must measure. Founder time is a real acquisition cost.

Enrolled
50 distinct consenting pilot users who begin onboarding.
Later-day useful action
Activation: a meaningful action on a later day within the first 14 days, such as editing or completing a real entry. Generating an app or passively opening a page does not count. Target: 30/50.
Week-four active
At least one meaningful action during days 22–28. Target: 15/50. All 50 enrolled users stay the denominator for activation and week-four use.
Reported alongside
Trial expiry (day 14 changes who has access), paid access, and prompted versus unprompted returns, next to week-four use. Nonuse is not all attributed to disengagement.
Learning decision
Continue this wedge if activation is at least 30/50 and week-four use at least 15/50. Revise and retest if week-four use is 10–14/50, or if activation is below 30/50 despite week-four use of at least 15/50. Reconsider the wedge if week-four use stays below 10/50 after core failures are corrected. A broken journey first calls for a repair, not a market conclusion.

The first recurring use case. Narrow enough to test: students with real follow-ups they want to manage. “Bring one follow-up you don’t want to lose. Turn it into a personal tool and use it again next week.” The wider vision still includes hobbies, curiosity, personal projects and everyday needs. The discovery question did not prove demand for this use case; this pilot tests it. It is not a decision to reduce Ori to a CRM.

Why this audience. Curious students combine academic work, projects, clubs, career opportunities and personal interests: a plausible starting population for tools that carry context between activities. The 21/26 discovery supports investigating this local need. Optional context [R3]: Pew’s 2026 survey found that 61% of U.S. adults ages 18–29 have ever used ChatGPT. That supports familiarity with conversational AI, not demand for Ori or a student market size.

The next 50. Test whether useful app blueprints and personal referrals can recruit a second cohort with less founder help, comparing invitations, enrollment, useful actions, retained use and founder minutes per user. Sharing a blueprint must never share the creator’s private records. A proposed growth mechanism.

What is counted

The founder’s original ambition was 100,000 downloads by September 2027. The scale and the date stay; the measured quantity matches how Ori is distributed. While Ori is web-first, the chart counts cumulative distinct registered users. A website registration is not a download; if a native download or install channel exists, verified downloads are shown separately. Page views, generated personal apps and multiple devices are not counted as distinct people.

The burden of proof

From 50 at the end of January to 100,000 at the end of September is a 2,000-fold increase in eight months: about 159% compounded monthly growth. The last two months alone would need 75,000 more registrations, about 1,230 a day. That is what the targets require, not a claim that the current network can deliver it.

How the channels branch

UIUC first, with founder-led onboarding. After retention is demonstrated, two possible paths:

  • Other colleges. Recruit student hosts through founder and club-leader introductions; run a small trial cohort; compare activation, retention and founder time against UIUC.
  • Young urban professionals. Begin with UIUC alumni and relevant professional communities in Chicago, then consider other cities where comparable access develops: busy people who want room for interests, relationships and personal projects. A proposed channel, not an existing partnership or a proven market.

Each path needs a concrete invitation: bring one recurring need or interest and leave with something useful. Referrals and shared app blueprints, with personal records excluded from sharing, are acquisition mechanisms to test. No viral loop or referral rate is claimed.

Expansion gates

Gates weigh more than geography. Start small at a second campus or alumni group and compare useful returns, conversion, acquisition effort and support costs. If behavior weakens outside the founder’s network, improve the offer before expanding broadly. Large-scale acquisition needs channels and resources beyond 150 warm invitations, and YC acceptance is not assumed to supply them.

YC ambition

“Application ambition: YC Summer 2027.” An ambition, kept apart from customers and partners: no application decision, acceptance, backing or endorsement is implied. YC is a possible accelerator for the business, not the customer outcome or the reason the growth target becomes possible. Summer 2027 is the proposed primary milestone because it gives time to produce meaningful operating evidence. Winter 2027 is January–March 2027; its regular deadline was November 2, 2026, although late applications were still accepted when checked on November 15, 2026. Winter 2027 does not follow Summer 2027, and no Summer 2027 application deadline is stated here [Y1, Y2].

8. Business model and economics questions

The offer. 14 days free → $7.50/month for two months → $15/month thereafter. A proposed pricing experiment: a free trial, not a permanent free tier. No other price, unlimited AI usage or automatic billing flow is proposed, and this pitch implements no billing. Normal subscription access rules will be stated clearly when billing is actually implemented.

The intended trial. One useful app, a real action and a later-day return. Upgrading means choosing continued usefulness: the hope is that Ori becomes part of someone’s routine. No manipulative reminders, hidden charges, or making records inaccessible to force payment.

Why someone would return, and pay. A useful app already fits an interest or recurring task. Personal records make the next visit more valuable. The workspace can adapt when the person’s needs change. That is the hypothesis, not validated willingness to pay.

What we measure. Useful return → actual paid conversion → renewal → cost to serve. A business funnel, not a replacement for the five stages.

Economics questions

  • Trial conversion: actual first payments divided by trial starters whose trial window has finished. Incomplete cohorts are reported separately.
  • Retention: meaningful weekly actions, with access and payment status visible. Day-14 expiry affects how week-four use is read.
  • Full-price evidence: the first $15 charge is distinct from the next $15 renewal. An eight-week pilot does not establish full-price renewal under this pricing ladder.
  • Serving cost: model calls, retries, hosting and storage, and support. Free-trial users incur costs even when they never subscribe.
  • Acquisition cost: founder time and cash by channel. Relationships do not make acquisition free.
  • Margin: net collected revenue against actual serving costs before claiming profitability or a gross-margin percentage. A token-only estimate is not a complete cost.

The claim is “we have designed a measurable business experiment”, not “we already know people will pay”. The eight-week pilot can test activation, week-four use, introductory payment and cost; we follow the cohort through the first $15 charge and the next $15 renewal, and neither is a result before it happens.

Scale arithmetic, a scenario only. 1,000 retained subscribers paying $15 would produce $15,000 of monthly subscription revenue before costs. Not a forecast, a market size or an achieved target.

Three examples worth explaining if asked.

  1. Reuse instead of clutter. A new follow-up should go into the right existing People app. Success is a useful action, not another generated icon.
  2. Preserve the person’s progress. Changing an app should never lose an observation, a session or a task. Planned duration and measured activity stay distinct.
  3. Make the experiment behavioral. Ask whether someone returns on a later day and completes a real action, then whether they pay and renew. Generated apps and enthusiastic reactions are weaker evidence.

What we attribute to Velocity. It informed the decision workflow, capability mapping, failure cases, data-preservation requirements and the demo and validation plan: it shaped, specified and helped us test assumptions. It did not build or validate Ori. The original research handoff did not edit or test the product repository; current implementation needs its own evidence. We have not measured a time saving. AI-generated research and fictional fixtures do not validate customer behavior; human observation and actual use do.

A useful next run. Take real prototype recordings and observed pilot sessions, compare them against the existing criteria, and revise one important assumption. The handoff identifies this as a pending Prompt C; it is not counted as completed.

11. About Ori’s expressions

One small companion sits in the same upper corner of every scene, larger only at the opening (scene 1) and the spark reveal (scene 5). It uses the four expressions of Ori’s identity board, drawn from the product’s canonical art: Listening (scenes 3, 5 and 11), Thinking (scenes 2, 4, 10, 13 and 14), Found something (scenes 6, 9 and 12) and Ready (scenes 1, 7, 8 and 15). It crossfades gently between expressions and stays still under reduced motion.

These expressions are storytelling cues. They do not imply that Ori detects or senses anyone’s emotions. In the product, the companion’s mood follows what the app is doing, such as thinking while a request is pending, never a reading of the person.
