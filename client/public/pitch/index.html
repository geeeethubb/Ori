2. Customer discovery, the longer story

Expanded narration for a longer telling or for questions. The friend’s words are paraphrased; the reel and the note are illustrative.

We asked people whether they struggle to turn their ideas into something consistent. Twenty-one of twenty-six said yes.

But one response made us rethink where the problem begins.

A friend explained that she sometimes struggles to pinpoint what the idea even is.

Maybe it starts with an Instagram reel you saved because you wanted to try something. Or a note that starts, “Why do I never…” and stops there.

You can bring that to Ori.

Ori is your companion for holding onto those sparks, connecting them with what you’ve shared, and helping you explore what they could become. As you react, clarify, and change your mind, Ori helps shape a next step around you.

You don’t need a fully formed idea to begin. You just need somewhere to bring it.

What changed because of discovery. Our design starts before a precise prompt or app specification. The friend gave us a reason to look closely at the first transition. We have not measured how many people stall at each stage.

The number. 21 of 26 said yes (21/26 = 80.8%; the slide shows the count, not the percentage). Informal conversations with 26 UIUC students, founder-reported: a convenience sample of people the founder could reach, not a random or representative one. An early local signal, not a campus-wide estimate, not a figure for college students in general, and not 21 users, paying customers or product-market fit.

3. The five stages, complete

Slide 3 shows the five stage names, joined by one path with Ori at its center. The full framework:

StageIn shortThe human taskOri’s intended role
1. Feel itA feelingSomething interests or bothers you.Capture the spark with the context you choose to share.
2. Name itA clearer needPut that interest or need into words.Ask a useful question; reflect possible meanings.
3. Imagine itPossibilitiesWork out what an ideal outcome would feel like.Explore several useful directions.
4. Shape itYour toolTurn the desired outcome into a concrete tool or workflow.Propose a personal app or adapt an existing one.
5. Build and iterateUse and evolveUse it, learn, and change it.Support useful actions and revisions while retaining progress.

“Ori helps you through all five.” The connected path on the slide is the product journey: one companion from the first feeling to an app you keep using. Stage five loops back (“And keeps growing with you”): using something changes what you want. Inside stage five, the demo shows the small loop of a change you ask for: Tell Ori → Review → Update.

The ideal solution. You should be able to arrive uncertain, explore without committing, turn useful context into something actionable, and keep changing the result without starting over. Ori reduces the work around acting; the person still chooses, practices, explores, and does the real-world work.

Not every spark needs an app. Sometimes the right outcome is saving a thought, asking a question, or using a tool that already exists. That choice is part of Ori’s intelligence.

A product framework, not a scientifically validated behavioral model and not a guarantee of habit formation.

5. How a personal app keeps your progress

Light, Riff and People look and work differently (a gallery of observations, a practice session, a follow-up queue), but they share one foundation of supported capabilities:

  • Useful actions. Each app does something real for its activity, such as saving an observation, timing a practice session or completing a follow-up, rather than only showing a generated screen.
  • Durable records. What a person saves belongs to their app and stays when they come back.
  • Careful revisions. A change to an app’s design must keep the records that make it useful: the same photo and note before and after the change on slide 6.

Implementation foundations, not the pitch’s story. What counts as implemented rests on current code and a working recording.

What is counted

The founder’s original ambition was 100,000 downloads by September 2027. The scale and the date stay; the measured quantity matches how Ori is distributed. While Ori is web-first, the chart counts cumulative distinct registered users. A website registration is not a download; if a native download or install channel exists, verified downloads are shown separately. Page views, generated personal apps and multiple devices are not counted as distinct people.

The burden of proof

From 50 at the end of January to 100,000 at the end of September is a 2,000-fold increase in eight months: about 159% compounded monthly growth. The last two months alone would need 75,000 more registrations, about 1,230 a day. That is what the goals require, not a claim that the current network can deliver it.

How the channels branch

UIUC first, with founder-led onboarding. After retention is demonstrated, two possible paths:

  • Other campuses. Recruit student hosts through founder and club-leader introductions; run a small trial cohort; compare activation, retention and founder time against UIUC.
  • Young professionals. Begin with UIUC alumni and relevant professional communities in Chicago, then consider other cities where comparable access develops: busy people who want room for interests, relationships and personal projects. A proposed channel, not an existing partnership or a proven market.

Each path needs a concrete invitation: bring one recurring need or interest and leave with something useful. Referrals and shared app blueprints, with personal records excluded from sharing, are acquisition mechanisms to test. No viral loop or referral rate is claimed.

Expansion gates

Gates weigh more than geography. Start small at a second campus or alumni group and compare useful returns, conversion, acquisition effort and support costs. If behavior weakens outside the founder’s network, improve the offer before expanding broadly. Large-scale acquisition needs channels and resources beyond 150 warm invitations, and YC acceptance is not assumed to supply them.

YC Fall 2027: an application goal

An ambition, kept apart from customers and partners: no application decision, acceptance, backing or endorsement is implied. YC is a possible accelerator for the business, not the customer outcome or the reason the growth goal becomes possible. Fall 2027 is the proposed milestone because it gives time to produce meaningful operating evidence. 9. Pricing and economics questions

Proposed pricing. 14 days free → $7.50/month for 2 months → $15/month thereafter. A proposed pricing experiment: a free trial, not a permanent free tier. No other price, unlimited AI usage or automatic billing flow is proposed, and this pitch implements no billing. Normal subscription access rules will be stated clearly when billing is actually implemented.

Why someone would keep it. Personal fit: a useful app already fits an interest or recurring task. Your progress: personal records make the next visit more valuable. Room to grow: the app can change when the person’s needs change. That is the hypothesis, not validated willingness to pay.

The intended trial. One useful app, a real action and a later-day return. Upgrading means choosing continued usefulness: the hope is that Ori becomes part of someone’s routine. No manipulative reminders, hidden charges, or making records inaccessible to force payment.

What we measure. Useful return → actual paid conversion → renewal → cost to serve. A business funnel, not a sixth stage.

Economics questions

  • Trial conversion: actual first payments divided by trial starters whose trial window has finished. Incomplete cohorts are reported separately.
  • Retention: meaningful weekly actions, with access and payment status visible. Day-14 expiry affects how week-four use is read.
  • Full-price evidence: the first $15 charge is distinct from the next $15 renewal. An eight-week pilot does not establish full-price renewal under this pricing ladder.
  • Serving cost: model calls, retries, hosting and storage, and support. Free-trial users incur costs even when they never subscribe.
  • Acquisition cost: founder time and cash by channel. Relationships do not make acquisition free.
  • Margin: net collected revenue against actual serving costs before claiming profitability or a gross-margin percentage. A token-only estimate is not a complete cost.

The claim is “we have designed a measurable business experiment”, not “we already know people will pay”. The eight-week pilot can test activation, week-four use, introductory payment and cost; we follow the cohort through the first $15 charge and the next $15 renewal, and neither is a result before it happens.

Scale arithmetic, a scenario only. 1,000 retained subscribers paying $15 would produce $15,000 of monthly subscription revenue before costs. Not a forecast, a market size or an achieved target.

Three examples worth explaining if asked.

  1. Reuse instead of clutter. A new follow-up should go into the right existing People app. Success is a useful action, not another generated icon.
  2. Preserve the person’s progress. Changing an app should never lose an observation, a session or a task. Planned duration and measured activity stay distinct.
  3. Make the experiment behavioral. Ask whether someone returns on a later day and completes a real action, then whether they pay and renew. Generated apps and enthusiastic reactions are weaker evidence.

What we attribute to Velocity. It informed the decision workflow, capability mapping, failure cases, data-preservation requirements and the demo and validation plan: it shaped, specified and helped us test assumptions. It did not build or validate Ori, establish demand or prove willingness to pay, and its proposed tests are not passed tests. The original research handoff did not edit or test the product repository; current implementation needs its own evidence. We have not measured a time saving. AI-generated research and fictional fixtures do not validate customer behavior; human observation and actual use do.

A useful next run. Take real prototype recordings and observed pilot sessions, compare them against the existing criteria, and revise one important assumption. The handoff identifies this as a pending Prompt C; it is not counted as completed.

12. About Ori’s expressions

The companion uses the four expressions of Ori’s identity board, drawn from the product’s canonical art: Listening (scenes 2, 4 and 10), Thinking (scenes 12 and 13), Found something (scenes 5, 7, 8, 9 and 11) and Ready (scenes 1, 3, 6 and 14). It sits in the same upper corner of most scenes, larger at the opening, and becomes part of the composition where the story centers on it (scenes 3, 4, 7, 8, 9 and 14; on scene 7 it marks the “Ori connects the dots” step). The closing scene (15) has no companion. It crossfades gently between expressions and stays still under reduced motion.

These expressions are storytelling cues. They do not imply that Ori detects or senses anyone’s emotions. In the product, the companion’s mood follows what the app is doing, such as thinking while a request is pending, never a reading of the person.
